01 / The basics

What actually is a black hole?

It is not a cosmic vacuum cleaner roaming around space. From far away, its gravity behaves like the gravity of any other object with the same mass. The strange part happens when matter gets extremely compact.

01 — GRAVITY

Space-time bends

In Einstein's theory of general relativity, mass and energy curve space-time. A black hole is a region where that curvature creates a one-way boundary.

02 — THE BOUNDARY

The event horizon

This is the point of no return. It is not a solid surface; crossing it means every possible future path leads deeper inward.

03 — THE SHADOW

Why it looks dark

We cannot receive light from inside the horizon. Hot gas outside it can glow brilliantly, creating a bright ring around a dark central shadow.

Important distinction: the bright ring seen in black-hole images is glowing material and lensed light around the black hole—not the event horizon itself.
02 / Anatomy

Take a closer look

Different parts of the system tell scientists different things about the black hole and its surroundings.

THE CENTRAL REGION

Horizon and interior

  1. Event horizon: the boundary beyond which escape is impossible.
  2. Singularity (classical theory): general relativity predicts a breakdown of its equations at the centre. A complete quantum-gravity description remains unknown.
  3. Spin: real black holes can rotate, affecting nearby space-time and the motion of surrounding matter.
THE VISIBLE ENVIRONMENT

Gas, light and jets

  1. Accretion disk: gas and dust heat up as they orbit and fall inward, sometimes becoming extremely bright.
  2. Gravitational lensing: gravity bends light, letting us see distorted views of material behind the black hole.
  3. Jets: some systems launch powerful particle streams from the region around the black hole, guided by magnetic fields.
03 / Cosmic scale

Black holes come in different sizes

Scientists classify them mainly by mass. The details of how the largest ones formed are still being investigated.

STELLAR-MASS

Born from stars

Form when the core of a sufficiently massive star collapses at the end of its life. They typically have a few to many tens of times the Sun's mass.

INTERMEDIATE

The middleweights

These have hundreds to hundreds of thousands of solar masses. Evidence exists for candidates, but their origin and population remain active research topics.

SUPERMASSIVE

Galactic giants

Millions to billions of solar masses. Many large galaxies host one; Sagittarius A* sits at the centre of our Milky Way.

04 / How we know

Discoveries that changed astronomy

Black holes are detected through their effects on nearby matter, light, and space-time—not by seeing light escape from inside them.

2015

Gravitational waves

LIGO detected ripples in space-time from two merging black holes, opening a new way to observe the universe.

2019

First black-hole image

The Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released an image of the shadow of the supermassive black hole in galaxy M87.

2022

Our galaxy's black hole

The Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released an image of Sagittarius A*, the black hole at the centre of the Milky Way.

These are milestones, not the only ways black holes are studied. Astronomers also track stars orbiting invisible massive objects and observe X-rays from hot gas.

05 / Myth check

Fact or fiction?

“Black holes suck everything in.”

Fiction. At a safe distance, an object can orbit a black hole just as it could orbit another object of the same mass. You must get very close for the extreme effects to dominate.

“We can study them without seeing inside.”

Fact. Their influence on stars, hot gas, gravitational waves, and light gives us powerful evidence about their mass, spin, and surroundings.

“They are holes through space.”

Not in the usual sense. The name describes a region from which light cannot escape, not an empty tunnel with a known destination.

“Time behaves normally near one.”

Not quite. Strong gravity affects how time is measured by observers in different locations; this is a prediction of general relativity.

07 / Curious minds

Questions people often ask

Would the Sun become a black hole?

No. The Sun does not have enough mass to collapse into a black hole at the end of its life. It is expected to become a white dwarf after its giant phases.

Can scientists see a black hole directly?

They can image the black hole's shadow against glowing surrounding material, and measure its effects. The event horizon itself does not emit light for us to see.

What would happen to someone falling in?

The experience would depend on the black hole's mass and the path taken. Differences in gravity across an object can stretch it—a process nicknamed “spaghettification.” This is a theoretical explanation, not a safe or survivable journey.

Do black holes last forever?

According to theoretical predictions, black holes can very slowly lose energy through Hawking radiation. For astrophysical black holes, this process would take far longer than the current age of the universe.
